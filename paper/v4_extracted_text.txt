##### PAGE 1 MultiCardioFusion: A Multimodal Deep Learning Framework for Heart Disease Prediction via Clinical Feature and ECG Signal Fusion Dhanvanth Yugandhar Raj Department of Computer Science, VIT University | Biomedical AI Research Group Abstract Cardiovascular disease is the world's foremost preventable killer, claiming approximately 17.9 million lives annually according to WHO estimates [36]. Most published machine learning work on cardiac screening draws from a single data source- either structured clinical measurements or electrocardiogram (ECG) signals- rarely combining both. This paper introduces MultiCardioFusion , a framework that fuses 13 clinical variables from the UCI Cleveland Heart Disease dataset with 568 statistical ECG features extracted from PTB- XL Lead- II signals using the tsfresh library [10]. Three fusion architectures are evaluated: (i) direct feature- level concatenation fed into tree ensembles, (ii) a soft- voting ensemble pooling probability outputs from four independently tuned classifiers, and (iii) a dual- branch neural network in which a cross- attention mechanism allows the ECG branch to query the clinical branch. On an 80/20 stratified hold- out, the soft- voting configuration achieves AUC = 0.9535 and accuracy = 88.52%- a 7.04 percentage- point gain over the best clinical- only baseline. SHAP analysis identifies thalach, oldpeak, ca, and cp as the dominant predictors, consistent with established cardiology practice. Statistical tests indicate the fusion benefit is practically meaningful, though the modest cohort size limits formal inferential significance. Keywords - multimodal learning, heart disease prediction, ECG feature extraction, tsfresh, SHAP explainability, XGBoost, ensemble fusion, clinical decision support, cross- attention, SMOTE. I. INTRODUCTION Heart disease remains the leading cause of mortality, motivating research and development into automated diagnosis and early detection tools. In clinical diagnosis, cardiologists rely on multiple sources of information, including blood tests, patient history, genetical history, imaging, and electrocardiogram are inferred together to provide a comprehensive diagnosis. However, the overwhelming majority of the published machine learning model to diagnose heart diseases rely on unimodal data source. The models reliant on unimodal data sources lacks the robustness and comprehensive clinical context to make a clinical diagnosis. Bridging the gap between multimodal clinical diagnosis workflows and current AI practice is crucial next step for advancing the medical machine learning. The field has approached cardiac classification predominantly through two distinct avenues. Structured tabular datasets, such as the UCI Cleveland Heart Disease dataset has been the standard testbed for clinical- feature- based cardiac classification since Detrano et al. published it in 1989 [1]. The accuracies reported have increased from 77% through early logistic regression model to over 93% through deep learning and ensemble methods [2] [3] [4]. Simultaneously, large scale waveform datasets, such as the PTB- XL benchmark dataset comprising of 21,837 ten- second 12- lead electrocardiogram recordings annotated by clinical experts, has been established as the foundational dataset for developing and evaluating machine learning models for clinical diagnosis leveraging electrocardiogram signal understanding [6]. The PTB- XL have advanced raw electrocardiogram signal processing exponentially over the decades. Despite extensive research in this domain, an interpretable prediction pipeline that leverages the advantages of clinical feature datasets and electrocardiogram signals remains scantly explored area. The Implementation of multimodal fusion in this domain presents major engineering obstacles. Firstly, The UCI Cleveland Heart Disease dataset and PTB- XL benchmark dataset is published by different institution in different eras, collected from different set of patient population making direct concatenation of data impossible. Secondly, there is a severe dimensionality mismatch between the high dimensionality feature space of electrocardiogram and compact 13- column clinical feature table, requiring intensive data engineering. Finally, clinical diagnosis model requires strict auditability; Hence, any advanced multimodal fusion models must be engineered to ensure a high degree of interpretability for medical professionals. 
##### PAGE 2 To address these challenges, we propose the MultiCardioFusion, an explainable and multimodal diagnostic prediction pipeline. MultiCardioFusion derives ECG- proxy features from Cleveland's own ECG- related measurements (restecg, oldpeak, slope, thalach, exang) and strengthens them with engineered interaction and nonlinear terms. This approach effectively captures electrocardiogram signal while eschewing the cross- cohort alignment issue altogether. The pipeline then compares feature- level tree ensembles, a soft- voting multi- classifier ensemble, and a cross- attention dual- branch neural network. To ensure real- world credibility, we integrate TreeSHAP throughout the diagnostic prediction pipeline to maintain transparency and clinical interpretability at both global (which features matter most on average) and local (why did the model flag this particular patient) levels. This works principal contributions include: ● A Novel Feature Engineering Framework: The derivation of ECG- proxy features that bridge the dimensionality and pairing gaps between standard clinical tables and electrophysiological signals. ● The MultiCardioFusion Pipeline: A comprehensive evaluation of multimodal fusion techniques, introducing the MCF- BiModNet architecture. ● Clinical Interpretability: The application of SHAP- based analysis to ensure both global feature importance and local, patient- specific prediction transparency. Clinical Feature- Based Cardiac Prediction Predictive modeling on the UCI Cleveland dataset has evolved from early probabilistic scoring [1] to advanced ensemble and deep learning techniques improving the accuracy over 90% in past three decades [2] [3]. Remarkable milestones include Arabasadi et al.’s [2] optimized neural network using genetic algorithm (93.1%), Mohan et al.’s [4] hybrid HRFLM architecture (88.4%), and Haq et al.’s [3] SVM classifier with prior aggressive feature selection, which achieved an Area Under the Curve (AUC) of 0.950, which is the highest validated metric currently reported on the unimodal dataset. However, a pervasive limitation across this literature is the dataset’s restrained size (N=303). The standard 10- fold cross validation method produces test folds which contain approximately 30 samples, resulting in accuracy estimates which show high variability and confidence intervals that reach their widest possible range. The 98.6% accuracy reported by Ga et al. [8] using bagging and principal component analysis requires careful evaluation because the research presents itself as near- perfect predictive performance. The absence of threshold- agnostic evaluation metrics together with missing details about cross validation stratification methods in those studies restricts the ability to reproduce results while decreasing the clinical usefulness of the research. B. ECG Signal Analysis The PTB- XL dataset introduction brought a major breakthrough to ECG research which enabled the development of complex neural network systems that Khan used to create a model which achieved 90.5% accuracy in detecting multiple cardiac abnormalities. The research by Hannun and his colleagues demonstrated that a 34- layer residual network system could deliver cardiologist- level accuracy in arrhythmia classification through the use of single- lead Holter monitor data. Even so, depending just on ECG readings leaves gaps, since such systems ignore broader physiological context. Electrical patterns alone miss changes in key blood markers as well as structural issues like artery clogging deposits. Without including these factors, prediction accuracy suffers. Merging structured patient histories

Multimodal and Cross- Modal Fusion Multimodal fusion is now seen as the next phase in the evolution of artificial medical intelligence. Litjens et al. [11] conducted a survey of deep learning techniques applied to radiology which have been able to effectively combine medical imagery with accompanying metadata streams; while Attia et al. [12] showed that the ability to include demographic data (baseline variables) in conjunction with ECG waveform data has allowed for effective screening for atrial fibrillation. Zhang et al. [13] proposed a multimodal/ multi- perspective framework for the combination of continuous ECG signal data and standardized clinical note data or structured clinical notes. The biomedical AI systems have started to integrate cross- attention mechanism to their architecture, which were once exclusive to transformer models. This ensures a more structured and definitive approach to facilitate interaction between the two independent modalities. Moreover, Ribeiro et al. [22] demonstrated that multi- lead ECG embeddings effectively understands the trends and anomalies influencing a heart risk. The current literature is lacking, and there are no previous research projects that have integrated both the Cleveland clinical benchmark dataset and the ECG feature dataset derived from PTB- XL within a common unified evaluation framework with SHAP annotation. The MultiCardioFusion architecture proposed in this study addresses the relationship between Multimodal Feature Engineering and Patient Level Interpretability in a comprehensive manner through the use of rigorous methodologies and techniques. D. Explainability in Clinical AI SHAP is extensively used for clinical AI systems trained using structured data due to its algorithmic simplicity and consistency [15]. Additionally, TreeSHAP enables the fast and precise calculation of Shapley value for tree- based ensembles such as XGBoost [16]. Recent research has demonstrated [23] [24] that SHAP attributions correlate very well with professional medical diagnoses. While recent Cleveland Dataset studies have reported considerable high accuracies (e.G Arabasadi et al. [2] - 93.1% and Gao et al. [8] - 98.6%), they are not representative of the entire scenario. Due to the limited 303 patient ‘ceiling’ of the dataset, raw accuracy is very susceptible to fold- to- fold variance and stratification inconsistencies. Additionally, none of the studies reporting high accuracies reported the area under the curve; a threshold agnostic metric that is considered to be more stable for assessment of clinical risk. Currently, the highest single- modality AUC reported for this benchmark is 0.950, reported by Haq et al. [3] using aggressive feature selection methods. The MultiCardioFusion framework is engineered to addresses these key methodological gaps by combining clinical data with engineered ECG- proxy features. The framework is built robust to overcome single- modality limitations, combining improved predictive accuracy with rigorous validation and evaluation and per- patient SHAP- based explainability, which are absent in prior work. III. DATASETS AND PREPROCESSING A. UCI Cleveland Heart Disease Dataset The dataset for heart disease in Cleveland was obtained (UCI Repository, ID = 45) from the ucimlrepo API [38], to avoid the use of csv files which were subject to large amounts of manual error in previous studies. There are 303 records (patients) each with 13 features related to health in addition to a dependent variable (target) which indicates the severity of coronary artery narrowing in numbers from 0 to 4. This target variable was modified according to common practice, using binary classification (defined as y=1 if num >0 or y=0 if num=0), to produce 139 patients with coronary artery narrowing and 164 patients without coronary artery narrowing. The number of patients with coronary artery disease is slightly smaller than the number without coronary artery disease (54.1% vs. 45.9%); any resulting imbalances were addressed later with the use of SMOTE ( Synthetic Minority Over- sampling Technique ) [17]. Four columns contained missing values: ca (n = 4) and thal (n = 2). Median imputation was used over mean imputation because both variables are ordinal; the mean can give non- integer values outside the permitted range. Dropping rows was not considered given the small cohort. After imputation, all 13 features were standardised to zero mean and unit variance using StandardScaler, so that variables with wide numerical ranges- such as serum cholesterol in mg/ dL- do not disproportionately influence distance- sensitive classifiers. PTB- XL ECG Dataset PTB- XL [6] provides 21,837 ten- second 12- lead ECG recordings sampled at 500 Hz (with a 100 Hz downsampled version also available) from 18,885 patients recruited across multiple European clinical sites. Each recording is stored in WFDB binary format and tagged with one or more SCP diagnostic codes drawn from a 44- code vocabulary covering normal sinus rhythm, myocardial infarction, bundle branch blocks, and arrhythmia subtypes. For binary labelling, a record was designated normal (y = 0) only when the sole diagnostic code with likelihood greater than zero was NORM; all others mapped to abnormal (y = 1). This produced 9,111 normal and 12,726 abnormal recordings across the full dataset. To match the Cleveland cohort size, a stratified sample of 303 records (126 normal, 177 abnormal) was drawn using a fixed random seed (SEED = 42) throughout to ensure reproducibility. Lead- II was the sole channel used for feature extraction. Lead II is aligned with the heart's mean electrical axis, making P- wave and QRS morphology most legible from it, and it is the first lead clinicians consult on a rhythm strip. Signals were read with wfdb.rdsamp(), yielding arrays of shape (1000, 12); column index 1 (Lead II) was extracted, giving one 1000- sample vector per patient at 100 Hz. Figure 1 provides a three- part overview of the PTB- XL sample selected for this study. The panel on the left illustrates the balanced distribution of recordings, with 126 labeled as normal and 177 as abnormal after stratified sampling. In the middle, a scatter plot maps patient age against ECG diagnosis. While abnormal cases span all age groups, normal recordings are more common among younger individuals, reflecting the well- known increase in cardiovascular risk with age. The right- hand panel shows a short Lead- II trace from both a normal and an abnormal recording, covering the first three seconds of signal. This side- by- side view highlights the morphological differences that the tsfresh feature extraction method is designed to capture. Taken together, the panels confirm that the chosen PTB- XL subset is clinically reasonable and that Lead- II contains clear, visually distinguishable patterns between classes. ECG Feature Extraction Pipeline Each Lead- II vector was reformatted into the long- form DataFrame expected by tsfresh [10]: columns {id, time, value}, where id is the patient index and time runs from 0 to 999. The EfficientFCParameters suite was applied, computing 777 statistical descriptors spanning time- domain statistics (mean, variance, skewness, kurtosis, root mean square), autocorrelation coefficients (ACF at lags 1– 10, partial ACF), trend descriptors (OLS slope/ intercept, SPKT- Welch spectral density), entropy measures (approximate entropy, sample entropy), frequency- domain aggregates (binned FFT coefficients, spectral energy), and nonlinear indicators (c3 statistic, CID- CE, time- reversal asymmetry). 

##### PAGE 6 Feature extraction across 303 signals required approximately 87 seconds on a standard Colab CPU. A VarianceThreshold filter (threshold = 0.01) then discarded 209 features whose variance across the patient population was too low to carry discriminative information, leaving 568 retained descriptors. These were subsequently standardised with a second StandardScaler fitted exclusively on the ECG training partition. D. Multimodal Dataset Construction To sidestep the cross- cohort label- misalignment problem that would arise from naively pairing Cleveland rows with unrelated PTB- XL patients, a feature engineering strategy was adopted. ECG- proxy features were derived entirely from Cleveland's own ECG- linked columns- restecg, oldpeak, slope, thalach, and exang- and expanded with polynomial interactions, log- transforms, and ratio features (detailed in Section IV- E). This produced a 36- dimensional aligned multimodal matrix D3 in which every one of the 303 rows corresponds to a Cleveland patient with a verified Cleveland disease label. D1 (clinical only, 303 × 13) and D2 (PTB- XL tsfresh, 303 × 568) were retained as parallel unimodal benchmarks. IV. PROPOSED METHODOLOGY A. Overall System Architecture and Pipeline The MultiCardioFusion pipeline proceeds through eight distinct stages illustrated in Figure 1. The structured clinical tabular branch and the raw ECG time- series branch each undergo independent preprocessing and feature extraction before being merged into a unified fused representation. Three separate fusion architectures are then trained and evaluated in parallel, enabling a controlled comparison of how the choice of fusion mechanism itself affects downstream performance. ##### PAGE 7 B. MCF- BiModNet Algorithm The overall MCF- BiModNet algorithm is defined in Algorithm 1. The prediction process is broken down into four main phases: encodes the clinical and ECG inputs using two parallel dense branches, merges using cross- attention, uses smote to rebalance the two optimized predictions using soft- voting ensemble, and calculates the SHAP values that will be returned with the prediction. Mathematical Formulation of Key Operations The following subsections detail the three most important stages of the pipeline: C1. tsfresh Feature Extraction. Given a Lead- II signal x = [x ₁ , x ₂ , …, x_T] with T = 1000 time points, tsfresh computes a feature map f: ℝ ᵀ → ℝ ᴷ where K = 568 after variance thresholding. Key sub- computations include mean μ = (1/ T) Σ ᵢxᵢ; variance σ² = (1/ T) Σ ᵢ(xᵢ – μ)²; skewness γ ₁ = (1/ T) Σ ᵢ((xᵢ– μ)/ σ)³; autocorrelation at lag k: r(k) = Σ ᵢ(xᵢ– μ) (xᵢ ₋  – μ) / Σ ᵢ(xᵢ– μ)²; sample entropy SampEn(m,r,T) = –ln[Uᵐ(r) / U^(m– 1) (r)]; and variance threshold: feature f  is retained iff Var(f ) > τ = 0.01. C2. SMOTE Oversampling. For a minority- class sample xᵢ, SMOTE [17] generates a synthetic point x̃ = xᵢ + λ·( x̂ – xᵢ), where x̂ is a randomly selected neighbour among the k = 5 nearest minority- class neighbours and λ ~ Uniform(0,1). Interpolating within data helps. It is better than extrapolating beyond what we have seen .This way the statistical properties of the training data stays the same and the training distribution is also preserved . Therefore we stick to the data manifold. C3. Multi- Head Cross- Attention. The cross- attention layer computes Attention(Q, K, V) = softmax(Q·Kᵀ / √d_k)·V, where Q = W_Q·h_ecg, K = W_K·h_clin, V = W_V·h_clin. With 4 heads and d_k = 8, the multi- head output is MultiHead(Q,K,V) = Concat(head ₁ ,…,head_h)·W_O. The residual connection h_fuse = MultiHead(Q,K,V) + h_ecg preserves gradient flow and prevents the ECG branch from being entirely displaced by the attended clinical context. C4. Soft- Voting Ensemble. Each base classifier c_k outputs a class- probability vector P_k(x) ∈ [0,1]². The ensemble prediction is P̄ (x) = (1/ K) Σ_ k P_k(x), ŷ = argmax P̄ (x). Soft voting is preferred over hard voting because it incorporates each classifier's confidence rather than just its binary decision. For small datasets where individual classifiers may be miscalibrated, averaging probabilities reduces variance more reliably than majority vote. C5. SHAP Attribution. TreeSHAP [16] computes the Shapley value φ   for feature j as: φ   = Σ_{ S ⊆ F\ {j}} | S|! (| F|–| S|– 1)!/| F|! · [f(S ∪ {j}) – f(S)], where F is the full feature set and f(S) is the model output using only the features in subset S. TreeSHAP evaluates this in O(TL²) time per prediction (T = trees, L = max leaves), making it feasible on the 61- sample test set. D. Class Imbalance Correction via SMOTE 
##### PAGE 8 SMOTE was applied strictly after the stratified 80/20 split. Consequently, the 61- patient held- out test set contains only original patient records with no synthetic samples. For the 242- patient training partition, SMOTE was applied independently to each of the three datasets- clinical- only (D1), ECG- only (D2), and multimodal- aligned (D3)- producing balanced training distributions of {131 healthy, 131 disease} for D1 and D3, and {145 normal, 145 abnormal} for D2. Applying SMOTE before the split would constitute data leakage, artificially inflating validation scores- an error deliberately avoided here. E. Feature- Level Fusion and ECG- Proxy Engineering Rather than pairing Cleveland rows with unrelated PTB- XL patients, ECG- proxy features were engineered from Cleveland's own ECG- linked columns. Starting from the five raw ECG- adjacent variables (restecg, oldpeak, slope, thalach, exang), the following additional terms were computed: Polynomial: oldpeak², thalach² Interactions: oldpeak × slope, thalach × exang, oldpeak × thalach, restecg × oldpeak, slope × thalach Log- transforms: log(1 + oldpeak), log(thalach) - to stabilise right- skewed distributions Ratio features: thalach / (age+1) - normalise heart rate by age; oldpeak / (thalach+1) - relative ST depression per unit exercise capacity This produced a 23- column ECG- proxy block. Combined with the 13 original clinical features, the aligned multimodal matrix D3 is 303 × 36, with every row belonging to the same Cleveland patient and carrying the same Cleveland disease label. F. Soft- Voting Ensemble and Hyperparameter Tuning All four ensemble members were tuned via RandomizedSearchCV with 10- fold cross- validation on the SMOTE- balanced multimodal training set, optimising for accuracy. The search ran 50 iterations for XGBoost, 30 each for SVM and GradientBoosting, and 20 for Random Forest. Best configurations found: XGBoost- max_depth=3, learning_rate=0.01, subsample=1.0, colsample_bytree=0.7, min_child_weight=1, gamma=0 (CV Acc=0.8396); SVM- kernel=RBF, C=100, gamma=0.001 (CV Acc=0.8510); GBM- n_estimators=200, max_depth=3, learning_rate=0.01, subsample=1.0 (CV Acc=0.8167). The four tuned classifiers are then aggregated via the soft- voting scheme described in Section IV- C4. G. Dual- Branch Deep Learning with Cross- Attention The deep learning model implements the MCF- BiModNet architecture in TensorFlow/ Keras. Each branch processes its input through Dense(64, ReLU) → BatchNormalization → Dropout(0.4) → Dense(32, ReLU). The 32- dimensional outputs are reshaped into sequence tensors of shape (1, 32), and Keras MultiHeadAttention (num_heads=4, key_dim=8) is applied with the ECG branch as query and the clinical branch as key/ value. Following the residual connection and flattening, a Dense(16, ReLU) → Dense(1, sigmoid) head produces the final probability. Training uses Adam (lr=0.001) [31] with EarlyStopping (patience=15) and ReduceLROnPlateau (factor=0.5, patience=7), with best- epoch weights restored on termination. H. Evaluation Protocol Primary evaluation uses an 80/20 stratified hold- out split (242 train, 61 test), with SMOTE applied only to the training partition. AUC is the primary metric because it is threshold- independent and well- suited to imbalanced evaluation. Recall (sensitivity) is the key clinical metric: a missed heart disease case carries far greater cost than a false alarm. F1 and accuracy are reported for completeness. Results are also reported under 10- fold stratified cross- validation on all 303 samples to enable direct comparison with the Cleveland benchmark literature, which almost universally employs this protocol. Limitations This work has some limitations that need to be talked about.First the Cleveland dataset only has 303 patients. This is not a lot of people so it limits the analysis. Makes it more likely that the model will be overfitting.Also the data comes from one institution so the model has not seen patients from other groups like South Asian Sub- Saharan African or East Asian people. This is a problem because it makes us wonder if the model will work for people from areas. The way we used ECG signals from the PTB- XL dataset to replace the ECG recordings from the Cleveland patients is a practical solution. However it is not the solution because the two datasets are from different groups of people .The clinical records and ECG signals are not from the individuals, which is a problem .This means that we cannot fully measure the differences between the two datasets without having patient- level data that matches .If we had a dataset that included both variables and ECG recordings from the same patients it would make the model more reliable . And currently only Lead- II data was used for ECG feature extraction. A standard clinical ECG usually includes 12 leads which include limb leads (I, II, III, aVR, aVL, aVF) and chest leads (V1– V6) . Each lead looks at the hearts activity from a different angle and gives us more information for diagnosis. We chose Lead- II because it is commonly used and easy to understand [37]. It often gives us a picture of P waves QRS complexes and T waves and it is widely used for rhythm analysis. Using one lead also made the analysis less complicated and helped us test the idea of multimodal fusion. However using Lead- II limits the models ability to find problems with the heart that might be visible in other leads. Finally the tsfresh features we used are statistical descriptors.They are not, like the ECG biomarkers that doctors use, such as QTc interval, PR interval or QRS duration. These biomarkers are commonly used in cardiology and clinical guidelines. Although the statistical features help us understand the model better using established ECG biomarkers in the future would make the model more useful and practical for medicine. B. Future Work The highest- impact next step would be to replace tsfresh statistical descriptors with representations from a self- supervised ECG transformer encoder- models such as ST- MEM or MERL pre- trained on millions of recordings- then fine- tune the fusion head on the Cleveland task. Extending extraction to all 12 ECG leads with appropriate dimensionality reduction represents an additional high- value upgrade. On the architecture side, federated learning across multiple hospital systems would provide the larger, more diverse cohort needed to properly validate the fusion approach. Longer- term, extending from binary classification to five- class stenosis severity prediction (num ∈ {0,1,2,3,4}) would make the system directly actionable for treatment planning beyond screening. VIII. CONCLUSION This paper presented MultiCardioFusion, a multimodal cardiac screening framework that fuses 13 UCI Cleveland clinical features with 568 tsfresh ECG descriptors from PTB- XL under a unified, SHAP- annotated prediction pipeline. The MCF- BiModNet algorithm combines the two modalities through a cross- attention dual-

##### PAGE 14 branch architecture and aggregates four tuned classifiers via soft- voting. On a 61- patient held- out set the system achieves AUC = 0.9535 and recall = 92.86%- identifying 26 of 28 disease- positive patients- with a +7.04 pp accuracy gain over the clinical- only XGBoost baseline. What is most encouraging is not the raw performance figures but what the SHAP analysis reveals: predictions are driven by thal, ca, cp, and oldpeak- precisely the features a cardiologist would identify as strongest risk indicators. That alignment between learned attribution and established clinical knowledge lends credibility to the hypothesis that the model is capturing genuine physiological signal rather than statistical artefacts of a 303- sample dataset. The limitations are real- the cohort is small, the ECG alignment is cross- cohort, and statistical tests remain underpowered- and they are problems that more modelling sophistication alone cannot resolve. What is needed is a larger, ideally paired, multi- centre dataset. The contribution of this work is to establish a reproducible, interpretable baseline for multimodal cardiac fusion that future studies with richer data can build upon. REFERENCES [1] R.
